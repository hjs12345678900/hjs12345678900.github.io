GROUP GUIDE

How the work happens.

Asteroid occultation observing is time-critical, collaborative and learnable. This guide explains the measurement, equipment, fieldwork and how the group operates.

On this page The measurement Equipment Fieldwork How we organise

01 · The measurement

What an asteroid occultation tells us

An asteroid occultation occurs when an asteroid passes in front of a distant star. The star can disappear for only a few seconds, but accurately measured disappearance and reappearance times define a chord across the asteroid's projected silhouette.

One observing station measures one chord. Several stations distributed cross-track—perpendicular to the shadow's motion—sample different slices of the predicted path and can recover the asteroid's size and shape far more precisely. The timing also provides an accurate astrometric position and can improve its predicted orbit.

Why multiple stations matter

A miss is still data. It marks where the asteroid's shadow was not and can constrain the edge of the silhouette when combined with nearby positive detections.

02 · Shared equipment

Our telescopes

We provide occultation-ready telescopes, cameras and timing equipment, and train new participants to use the complete setup.

03 · Fieldwork

The observing site follows the shadow

Sometimes we travel to rural Victoria or interstate sites to capture valuable events. People opt in when an event, their availability and the weather line up; the team shares route planning, equipment transport and decisions in the field.

Some high-priority campaigns receive travel support connected with ESA or NASA programmes. Support depends on the event, but taking part never depends on being available to travel.

Travel is optional

No one is expected to travel. Event triage, preparation, photometry, data analysis, reporting and equipment development can all be done without joining a field trip.

04 · Group structure

One group. No fixed roles.

This is a volunteer collaboration rather than a hierarchy of permanent roles. It brings together University of Melbourne students and staff, members of the Society for Astronomy at Melbourne University (SAMU) ↗, and interested people from the wider community.

People can move between different parts of the work. New participants learn alongside previous observers, contribute where they are comfortable and, if they wish, follow an event from its first prediction through to the final report.

Who can join?
Anyone curious about contributing: students, staff and people outside the University are all welcome.
What matters most?
Reliability when you volunteer, patience, careful work and a willingness to learn.
What experience is expected?
None with telescope operation. We start with the setup.
